Security fundamentals for data engineers

Security in data work is mostly a few habits applied everywhere: know who is asking, give the minimum access, protect secrets, know where sensitive data lives, and keep a record. This page lays them out as a mind map so you can check a pipeline or platform branch by branch. It teaches principles; follow your organisation's policy and your platform's documentation for specifics.

The data physics

Five plain facts that explain almost everything below. Read these and the rest follows.

Every access is a question: who, what, why.Identity says who. Permissions say what. Purpose and audit say why, and prove it later.
Least privilege is the default.Start with no access and grant exactly what a role needs. Wide access is easy to give and very hard to take back.
Secrets are the keys to everything else.A password in code, a notebook or a log defeats every other control.
You cannot protect what you cannot find.Sensitive columns must be known, labelled and traced downstream, otherwise controls miss them.
Copies multiply risk.Every extra copy of sensitive data is another place to secure, audit and delete.

The mind map

The whole topic on one screen

Each card is a branch. Walk them in order when you are stuck: the question tells you what the branch is about, and the first check tells you where to look.

Security

1. Identity and access

Who is asking?

Single sign-on, service principals, groups, roles, least privilege, short-lived tokens

First check: Who can read this table?

2. Secrets

Where do the keys live?

Secret stores, rotation, no secrets in code or logs, managed identities

First check: Search code and logs for credentials

3. Protection in place

Is the data protected at rest and in motion?

Encryption, key management, TLS, private networking

First check: Public endpoints and unencrypted stores

4. Know the data

Which data is sensitive?

Classification, tagging, PII discovery, lineage, data contracts

First check: Is every sensitive column labelled?

5. Limit what is seen

Who sees which rows and columns?

Row filters, column masks, views, anonymisation, tokenisation

First check: What does a junior analyst see?

6. Prove it

Can we show what happened?

Audit logs, access reviews, lineage, change history, alerts

First check: Can you answer 'who read this last week'?

7. Share and retire safely

Where does data go, and when does it go away?

Sharing, extracts, retention, deletion, backup, environments

First check: Production data in test?

Identity and access

Who can do what

Most data breaches are access mistakes, not clever attacks: an over-wide group, a shared account, a forgotten service account.

Layers of access control. Each gate narrows what is visible; the audit log proves it.

PrincipleWhat it meansGood practiceCommon mistakeVerdict
Least privilegeOnly the access the task needsGrant on groups and roles, not individuals'Just give them admin to unblock'Always
Separate identities for people and jobsPipelines run as service identities, not as a personOne service principal per pipeline or domainJobs running under an engineer's loginAlways
Short-lived credentialsTokens that expireManaged identities, federated loginLong-lived tokens in configPursue
Access by group, reviewedMembership is the control pointPeriodic access reviewAccounts of leavers still activeAlways
Separation of dutiesBuilder is not approverReview before productionOne person writes and ships alonePursue for sensitive systems
Environment separationDev, test, prod have separate accessNo production write access from devSame credentials everywhereAlways

Secrets

Keys, passwords and tokens

Treat any credential that has been pasted in a chat, committed to code or printed in a log as compromised.

DoDo notWhy
Keep secrets in a secret store and fetch at run timeHard-code them in code, notebooks or configCode spreads; secret stores have access control and audit
Rotate on a schedule and on any suspicionKeep one key for yearsLimits the window of misuse
Use managed identities where the platform allowsShare passwords between systemsNo secret to leak
Scan code and logs for credentialsPrint connection strings in logsLogs are widely readable

FAQ

Questions people ask

What is the single biggest security habit for a data engineer?

Least privilege on service identities plus no secrets in code. Together they remove the most common causes of data exposure.

Do I need to encrypt data myself?

Mostly the platform does at rest and in transit. Your job is to confirm it is on, understand who holds the keys, and never turn it off to unblock a problem.

What is the difference between masking and removing a column?

Masking keeps the column but hides or alters values per user. Removing keeps the data out of reach entirely. Use masking when the shape is useful.

Why does lineage matter for security?

When a sensitive column is found, lineage tells you every downstream table, dashboard and export that now carries it.

Is this page legal or compliance advice?

No. It explains engineering principles. Follow your organisation's policy, local law and your platform's current documentation.

Does CostSherlock check security?

Only a few signals that overlap with cost and governance, such as unmanaged compute and unusual activity. It is not a security scanner.
