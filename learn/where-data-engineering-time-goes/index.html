Where data engineering time really goes, and how to win it back

Ask someone what a data engineer does and they say "writes pipelines". Watch one for a month and you see something else: most hours go to finding out what the source data means, chasing people, and making numbers match. This page shows where the time goes, why the meaning of source data sits at the root of it, and how to win the non-technical hours back.

~28%of a first build goes to understanding what the source data means. The biggest single share.
~12%goes to writing the pipeline code: the part on the plan.
~60%of a first build is non-technical work: meaning, definitions, access, reconciling, meetings.
Read this first: the numbers are a rounded field estimate from many data platform projects, not a measured study. Two public surveys are quoted below for comparison. Use the picture to start a conversation in your team, then measure your own.

In one picture

Code is the tip of the iceberg

A project plan lists the things you can see: ingest, transform, test, deploy. Underneath sits the work nobody plans for, and it is bigger.

What gets planned sits above the water. What takes the time sits below it.

The first build

Where the hours go when you onboard a source

Take one new source, say an order system, from first meeting to first trusted gold table: usually three to six months. Here is how the hours split.

Understanding what the source data means takes more than twice the hours of writing the code.

Why is meaning so expensive? Because the answers are rarely written anywhere. Which column is the real key? Does a cancelled order disappear or get a status? Is updated_at set by every change or only some? Which of the three date columns is the business date? Each answer needs a person, and each wrong guess is found late, when the numbers are already in front of a user.

After go-live

A year of running looks different

Once the pipeline runs, fixing bad data and reruns take the most hours. Hover a bar for its value.

Meaning work drops once the source is learnt, but quality fixes and reruns grow to two fifths of the time. Look at why they happen: a source team re-codes a status, adds a column, starts sending deletes differently, back-dates a correction. Those are changes in meaning that nobody announced. The technical-looking work is mostly meaning work arriving late.

Why meaning is the root

One misread code, two weeks of work

Most of the expensive hours on this page trace back to one small misunderstanding about the source. Here is a real-shaped example.

Reconciling, reworking and firefighting are often the bill for one question not asked early.

So the cheapest hour on any project is the one spent asking the source team the right questions before the first line of code. That is what the upstream questions and the source questionnaire are for.

The non-technical work

The non-technical work, and how to cut it

A data engineer cannot avoid non-technical work entirely; understanding the business data is part of the job. What you can avoid is doing it late, doing it again, and doing it alone. Three moves cover almost everything:

  1. Earlier. Ask before coding, request access on day one, agree the reconciliation target before building.
  2. Once. Write every answer where the next person will find it: in the metadata store next to the column, and in the semantic layer for definitions.
  3. By the right person. Chasing people, chairing meetings and settling definitions belong to an analyst, a technical anchor or the business owner. The engineer builds from written answers.

Hand each kind of work to the person whose job it is. The engineer is not the default for all of it.

Your turn

Five questions for your own team

  1. For your last source, how many weeks passed before you could say what one row means? Who answered, and where is the answer written now?
  2. Which report do your numbers have to match, within what tolerance, and who signed that off?
  3. Is there one named owner for each important definition, or does the engineer decide?
  4. How many hours last month went to reruns and fixes caused by a source change nobody announced?
  5. If your best engineer left tomorrow, which source meanings would leave with them?

FAQ

Questions people ask

What do data engineers spend most of their time on?

In our experience, understanding what the source data means: what a row is, the keys, the status codes, how deletes and dates work, how tables join. In a first build that is roughly a quarter to a third of the hours, more than writing code. Once a pipeline runs, fixing data quality problems and reruns take over, and many of those trace back to source meaning that changed or was misread.

Is the '80% of time is cleaning data' figure true?

It is folklore from data science surveys and rarely sourced well. Public surveys of data teams land nearer 40% on data quality work. The point stands: most hours go to making data right and understood, not to writing new pipelines.

How do I reduce the non-technical work as a data engineer?

Move it earlier and to the right person. Ask the source questions before coding, get access requested on day one, have a business owner sign off definitions and the reconciliation target, and write every answer down where the next person will find it. The work does not vanish, but it is done once instead of rediscovered.

Can AI understand source semantics for me?

It can profile data, read documentation and draft good questions quickly. It cannot know that status 'C' means Cancelled in this one system unless someone wrote it down. Use it to prepare; let a person who knows confirm.

Are these percentages measured?

No. They are a rounded field estimate from many data platform projects, shown to start a conversation. Measure your own: tag a month of your team's hours with these ten activities.
