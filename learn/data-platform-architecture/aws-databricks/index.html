The data platform on AWS and Databricks

The one-minute version

Your account, your data, one catalog

Databricks runs inside your own AWS account. Data stays in your S3 buckets, in your VPC, encrypted with KMS keys you own. Databricks brings the engine, and Unity Catalog (its one catalog for tables, files and models) governs who sees what, tracks lineage and records every access.

The design does not depend on Databricks. It follows the same specs and the same 22 cross-cutting concerns as the abstract architecture. This page is the binding: the one place where product names appear. That is what keeps you free to change a product later.

Most of the value is in the shared parts. Ingestion, metadata, quality, cost, testing and the conversation layer are built once and reused, so each new data product (OEE, warranty claim rate, dealer sell-out, demand forecast) costs less than the one before.

The big picture

One platform, from plant and business systems to answers

Read it left to right along the middle row: data moves one way, from the plants' and the business's systems to dashboards, Genie and AI. Above it, the control plane decides what runs, and agents may only suggest. Below it, the horizontal assets serve every layer, on an AWS and Databricks foundation you own. Governance runs down the right side.

The foundation

Accounts, network, storage and keys, before any pipeline

Before a single pipeline runs, the platform needs somewhere safe to live: AWS accounts to hold it, a private network, buckets for the data with keys you own, Databricks workspaces to work in, and one catalog that decides who sees what. Think of it as the land, the roads and the locks. Done right once, nobody thinks about it again; done wrong, every later layer inherits the problem.

The nine choices

DecisionOur pickWhy
AWS accountsOne AWS account per environment: dev, pre-prod, prod. All three sit under your AWS Organization and send logs to your central security and log accounts.A mistake in dev cannot touch prod, permissions stay simple, and each environment's bill is separate.
RegionMumbai (ap-south-1) is home. Hyderabad (ap-south-2) holds the recovery copy.Both are in India, so recovery does not move data out of the country.
EU plantsPlant and production data from EU plants comes to Mumbai too. Personal data about EU people (staff, dealers) is kept to the minimum, masked where possible.Press readings and scrap counts are not personal data. One platform costs half of two. A separate EU platform (Frankfurt) only if your legal team requires it.
Databricks workspacesThree workspaces (dev, pre-prod, prod) on one Unity Catalog. Each catalog is bound to its workspace, so prod tables are visible only in prod.Databricks allows one Unity Catalog per region, so the three share it; binding keeps the environments apart.
NetworkNo public IP addresses anywhere. Compute runs in your own VPC, across two availability zones. Databricks is reached through PrivateLink, never the open internet.This is the first thing a manufacturer's security team asks. Two zones keep the platform up if one data centre fails.
Storage and keysOne S3 bucket and one KMS key per catalog. A landing bucket per environment. Recipes get their own bucket and key with the tightest access.Recipes are the crown jewels: with their own key, revoking all access is one action, and every use of the key is logged.
ComputeServerless by default for SQL, jobs and pipelines. Classic compute (clusters inside your VPC) only where a job must reach a private system that serverless cannot yet reach.Starts in seconds, nothing to patch or size. Serverless private reach on AWS: check current status.
Sign-in and identityPeople sign in through your existing identity provider; groups sync to Databricks automatically (SCIM). Jobs run as service principals, never as a person.When someone leaves, one change removes all their access, and no job breaks because its owner left.
How it is builtTerraform builds all of the above from Git (AWS and Databricks providers). Pipelines are deployed separately with Databricks Asset Bundles.The whole foundation can be rebuilt the same way in every environment, and in Hyderabad after a disaster.

Built from code

The foundation is Terraform in Git, applied in five steps. The environments differ only in one settings file each, so dev, pre-prod and prod are the same platform at different sizes.

foundation/                     # Terraform, one folder per layer, applied in this order
  1-org/          accounts, guardrails (SCPs), CloudTrail to the log account
  2-network/      VPC per env, private subnets in 2 zones, endpoints (S3, STS, Kinesis), PrivateLink
  3-storage/      buckets and KMS keys per catalog, IAM roles Databricks may assume
  4-databricks/   workspaces, metastore, catalogs, workspace bindings, external locations
  5-identity/     SCIM groups, service principals, grants on catalogs
  envs/           dev.tfvars, preprod.tfvars, prod.tfvars, dr.tfvars   # the only differences

Folder layout of the foundation code. Pipelines are not here: they are deployed with Asset Bundles.

Recovery: if Mumbai is lost, the same Terraform with dr.tfvars builds the workspace in Hyderabad on the copied buckets. Target: gold tables back within a day (an RTO of 24 hours), losing at most a few hours of data. We rehearse it twice a year.

Metadata and ingestion on AWS

One metadata database, one ingestion framework

The Common Ingestion Framework is metadata-driven: adding a source is a few lines of YAML, not a new pipeline. It follows the Metadata DB Design for Data Engineering Platforms, already built in our framework. Full table definitions, the view and the reader code are on the metadata store page.

The tables

TableWhat it holdsWritten by
platform_configSettings for the whole platform: default storage paths, default quality action, budget limitsPlatform team, through YAML
source_masterOne row per source system: SAP S/4HANA, MES, historian, quality LIMS, warranty, dealer DMSThrough YAML
source_configKey-value settings per source, grouped (ingestion, quality, cost): connection secret reference, mover, scheduleThrough YAML
dataset_masterOne row per dataset (table) of a source, with its target schema and tableThrough YAML
dataset_configKey-value settings per dataset: load mode, keys, history (SCD) type, quality rules, PII tags, ownerThrough YAML
dataset_viewThe one view every pipeline reads: platform, then source, then dataset settings merged into JSON, the most specific winningGenerated
run_logOne row per run: dataset, status, row counts, cost, and a fingerprint of the exact settings usedPipelines
watermarkHow far each incremental load has gotPipelines

The framework also has a key registry (every allowed setting and its type), per-column settings, quality results and a PII policy table on the same database.

YAML in GitHub, and a SQL database

We need both, for different jobs. People write and approve settings as YAML in GitHub; a loader validates every key and writes them to the database, one database per environment (dev, test, prod) with the same schema. Pipelines only ever read the database.

YAML in GitHubSQL metadata database
What it holdsConfiguration: sources, datasets, load modes, keys, rules, owners, PII tags, budgetsThe same configuration loaded from Git, plus runtime state: watermarks, run log, quality results
Who writes itPeople, and agents, by pull requestThe loader writes configuration; pipelines write runtime state
Why we need itReview and approval, full history, easy rollback, the same file promoted dev → test → prodFast lookups, keys between tables, the merged view, many jobs writing at once
Do pipelines read it?NeverAlways

Two rules. Nobody edits configuration in the database by hand: the next load would overwrite it and Git would have no record. Runtime state (watermarks, run log) never goes into Git.

Which database on AWS

The metadata store needs keys between tables, fast small reads and writes, safe parallel writes from many jobs, and good JSON for the merged view. That is a relational database, and PostgreSQL fits the design as written.

Where it sits: in your AWS account, in a private subnet, in the same region as the Databricks workspace (Mumbai for India; Frankfurt or Ireland if EU data needs its own platform). The password lives in AWS Secrets Manager; Databricks classic compute reaches it over the VPC, and serverless compute through a private endpoint. A copy of run_log lands in Delta every day so dashboards and cost reports can join it.

Three schemas in one database

One PostgreSQL database per environment, split into three schemas so that permissions are simple: the loader may write only config and audit, pipelines may write only runtime, and dashboards read everything.

SchemaHoldsWho writesSize and retention
configWhat to do: platform_config, source_master, source_config, dataset_master, dataset_config, the key registry, quality rules, PII policy, and dataset_viewOnly the loader writes; pipelines readSmall; changes only through Git
runtimeWhat happened: run_log, step_log, error_log, dq_result, watermark, config_snapshotPipelines write; nobody elseGrows every run; monthly partitions, 13 months kept, older months copied to Delta
auditWho changed what: config_load_log (every loader run, Git commit, who approved, what changed)Only the loader appends; never updated or deletedSmall; kept for as long as audit rules require

Logging: every run can be followed and replayed

Every run writes to the runtime schema. One run id ties the rows together, and it is also stored on the Databricks job run, so the logs join with Databricks system tables (cost, lineage, audit) and with the quality results.

TableOne row perMain columns
run_logOne row per run of one datasetrun_id, dataset_id, Databricks job and run ids, status, started and ended, rows read / written / rejected, config fingerprint, Git commit, estimated cost
step_logOne row per step inside a run: land, bronze, silver, goldrun_id, step, status, timings, row counts in and out
error_logOne row per failurerun_id, step, error class (source down, schema change, bad data, bug), message, retry number, whether it was retried

The watermark: load only what is new, safely

A watermark is a bookmark: the last value loaded from a source (a timestamp, an id, a change position). The next run starts after it. The danger is moving the bookmark before the data is safely written, or never moving it after a crash. These rules prevent both.

1
Read Read the last good watermark (low) from runtime.watermark.
2
Fix the window Fix the upper end (high) at the start, e.g. the source's current maximum, minus a small safety gap. The run loads only rows above low and up to high, so it never chases a moving target.
3
Load safely Write to bronze with a key-based MERGE, so loading the same window twice never doubles a row.
4
Commit once Only after the data is committed: mark the run succeeded and move the watermark to high, in one database transaction.
5
Crash in between? The watermark did not move, so the next run reloads the same window, and the MERGE makes that harmless.
6
Late rows A look-back overlap (say 2 hours, set in dataset_config) re-reads a little of the last window; the MERGE drops the repeats.
7
Files and streams Auto Loader and streaming keep their own checkpoints; we record the checkpoint location, not a second watermark. CDC sources use the change position (LSN or SCN); SAP uses its delta token.
-- schema runtime: written only by pipelines
CREATE TABLE runtime.run_log (
  run_id          uuid PRIMARY KEY,
  dataset_id      uuid NOT NULL REFERENCES config.dataset_master(dataset_id),
  job_run_id      text,                    -- Databricks job run id, to join system tables
  status          text NOT NULL,           -- running, succeeded, failed, skipped
  started_at      timestamptz NOT NULL,
  ended_at        timestamptz,
  window_low      text,                    -- watermark this run started from
  window_high     text,                    -- watermark this run loaded up to
  rows_read       bigint, rows_written bigint, rows_rejected bigint,
  config_fp       text NOT NULL,           -- fingerprint of the merged settings
  git_commit      text,
  est_cost_usd    numeric(12,4)
);
CREATE TABLE runtime.watermark (
  dataset_id      uuid PRIMARY KEY REFERENCES config.dataset_master(dataset_id),
  wm_column       text NOT NULL,           -- e.g. updated_at, change sequence number
  wm_type         text NOT NULL,           -- timestamp, number, cdc_position, delta_token
  wm_value        text NOT NULL,           -- last value loaded successfully
  last_run_id     uuid REFERENCES runtime.run_log(run_id),
  updated_at      timestamptz NOT NULL DEFAULT now()
);

The two runtime tables most people ask about first. The full config schema is on the metadata store page.

Ingestion: movers bring data in, our lander loads it

Ingestion has two steps. A mover brings raw data from the source into an S3 landing folder; which mover is a setting in source_config, so it can change without code. Then one lander, our PySpark job on Databricks using Auto Loader, reads its settings from dataset_view, writes bronze with arrival details, records a run_log row and is safe to run twice. Lakeflow Jobs are generated from the metadata and deployed with Databricks Asset Bundles.

Orchestration: which service runs what, and when

One rule: one orchestrator owns each pipeline. Lakeflow Jobs owns everything from the landing folder onwards; AWS services only start the movers on the AWS side and hand over. Two schedulers for the same pipeline is the most common cause of runs that overlap or never start.

Data quality and profiling

Rules written once, checked at every hop

Two different jobs. Data quality rules are checks we write down: "every reading belongs to a known curing press", "scrap quantity is never negative". Profiling measures what the data actually looks like (how many blanks, how many different values, the smallest and largest) so we notice when it changes, and so we can find rules nobody thought to write. Both are horizontal assets: switched on for every dataset from the metadata, not coded per pipeline.

Which engine checks the rules

Rules are stored as data in the metadata store, and the engine sits behind our quality port (a fixed interface), so the engine can change without touching a single rule.

EngineUsed forVerdictWhy
DQX (Databricks Labs)Default for every PySpark step: the lander, bronze to silver, the gold recompute jobsPursueRules as data (YAML or a table), row-level and table-level checks, splits good and bad rows, has a profiler that suggests rules. Open source, so no lock-in. Labs project: check current status for support terms.
Lakeflow expectationsInside Declarative Pipelines (silver and gold tables built that way)PursueNative to the pipeline, results land in the pipeline event log, no extra code. We generate them from the same rules, so a rule is written once.
Plain Spark SQLFallback: any rule the two above cannot express, such as a reconcile across two tablesPursueA predicate in SQL: rows where it is true are the bad rows. This is how the framework's rules already work today.
Great Expectations, SodaNot usedDropGood tools, but a second rule language and a second results store. DQX plus expectations already cover the need inside Databricks.
AWS Glue Data QualityOnly if a team already uses it on Glue jobsDropRuns outside Databricks; results would not join our run id without extra work.

What is checked at each hop

Each layer checks what it promises, and nothing more. Bronze keeps data as it arrived, so the real cleaning checks start at silver.

HopWhat is checkedWhat happens to bad data
Landing to bronzeContract check: expected columns and types, file not empty, row count against the source's countStop on a contract break; nothing else is checked here, bronze keeps the data as it arrived
Bronze to silverRow rules: not null, ranges, allowed values, keys unique, references exist (press, plant, material)Bad rows go to the silver table's quarantine table; good rows carry on. Stop only if the bad share passes a limit
Silver to goldTable rules: totals reconcile with silver, no gaps in the press-hour grain, share of 'unknown' members under a limitStop: a wrong gold number is worse than a late one
Gold to semanticMetric checks: OEE between 0 and 100%, scrap rate against last month's bandWarn, and flag the metric on the dashboard

A rule file, for one table

Stewards write rules as YAML next to the dataset's settings. The same file switches profiling on.

# config/dq/silver_mes_press_reading.yaml  (one file per dataset and layer)
dataset: mes.press_reading
layer: silver
rules:
  - name: press_known
    check: press_id IN (SELECT press_id FROM prod_silver.mes.press)
    severity: error
    action: quarantine
  - name: cure_temp_in_range
    check: cure_temp_c BETWEEN 120 AND 220        # example range; the plant engineer owns it
    severity: error
    action: quarantine
  - name: not_in_future
    check: reading_ts <= current_timestamp() + INTERVAL 5 MINUTES
    severity: warning
    action: warn
  - name: bad_share_limit                         # table-level: stop if too much is bad
    check: quarantined_pct <= 2
    severity: error
    action: stop
profile:
  enabled: true
  when: daily
  scope: new_rows
  sample_pct: 10
  columns: all
  suggest_rules: true

Rules and profiling settings for curing-press readings in silver. Ranges are examples; the plant's process engineer owns the real ones.

In the same metadata database, split by the three schemas: rules and settings in config (changed only through Git), results in runtime. These tables already exist in our framework; on AWS they move to Aurora PostgreSQL.

Profiling: three tools, each where it pays

Profiling every table on every run costs compute for little gain. So the default profiler runs on new data, the managed monitor runs only on the tables the business watches most, and personal-data detection runs on the catalogs.

From profile to rule: a person always decides

1
Profile The profiler measures each column of new data and writes runtime.dq_profile with the run id.
2
Compare Each profile is compared with the last one. A big change (nulls jump from 0.1% to 8%, a new value in plant_code) raises a warning on the ops dashboard.
3
Suggest If suggest_rules is on, candidate rules are written as a pull request to the rules YAML, marked as suggested. They are never switched on automatically.
4
Approve The data steward for that source reviews the pull request, adjusts the limits and merges. The loader puts the rule in config.dq_rule.
5
Enforce From the next run the rule is checked at its hop; the result lands in runtime.dq_result, failing rows in quarantine.
6
Review Every week the steward looks at the quarantine tables, fixes the source or the rule, and releases or drops the parked rows.

Later, an agent: it may read the profiles and quarantine tables, explain what changed in plain words and draft the rule change. It still arrives as a pull request for the steward.

Data contracts: do we need them?

A data contract is a written agreement between the team that produces data and the teams that use it: which columns, what they mean, how fresh, how good, and who owns it. It is written as a YAML file in Git using the open standard ODCS (Open Data Contract Standard, version 3), so it is both readable and checkable by machine. The short answer: yes, at the two boundaries, and no, not everywhere.

WhereContract?Why
At sources we do not control (SAP, MES, quality LIMS, dealer DMS)YesTheir teams change systems on their own timetable; a contract turns a silent break into a clear alert with an owner.
At data products other teams use (OEE, scrap rate, warranty claims)YesConsumers need to know the columns, the meaning, how fresh and who to call; it is also what Genie and dashboards rely on.
Between our own internal layers (bronze to silver to gold)NoOur tests and quality rules already cover them; a contract there is paperwork.
A full contract platform or marketplace toolNot nowYAML in Git plus the checks we already run is enough to start; revisit when there are dozens of producing teams.

Nothing new has to be built to enforce them: the contract check in the lander, the quality rules, the freshness and volume alerts and Unity Catalog tags already do it. The contract simply makes the promise explicit and gives every alert an owner. The open-source datacontract command-line tool can lint ODCS files and test them against the data if a team wants it.

The right medallion

One medallion, two speeds

The medallion is the row of layers in the middle: bronze (raw), silver (clean) and gold (shaped for the business). Streaming does not need a second architecture. Nightly batches and continuous events flow through the same tables, with the same contracts and the same checks; only the speed differs.

Common mistakes, and the fix

1
Dashboards reading silver or bronze. Two numbers for the same metric. Fix: experiences read only gold through metric views; lineage is checked against the allowed paths.
2
A separate architecture for streaming. Twice the code and two answers. Fix: one medallion, two speeds: streaming tables in the same layers.
3
Dropping bad rows silently. Counts drift and nobody knows why. Fix: quarantine with a reason, and reconcile counts from source to gold.
4
Overwriting history in silver. Yesterday's report can no longer be explained. Fix: SCD Type 2 where the business needs history.
5
One notebook per source. A hundred sources become a hundred snowflakes. Fix: metadata-driven ingestion; a new source is a YAML entry.
6
Protecting recipes and personal data only in the dashboard. Anyone with SQL access sees them. Fix: tag in silver, use a restricted catalog and Unity Catalog column masks everywhere.

More detail, with one record followed through every layer: Medallion architecture, drawn right.

The right AI/BI

The semantic layer first, then the AI

Ask three tools for "scrap rate" and you can get three numbers. The fix is to define each metric once, as a metric view in Unity Catalog, and make every front end read it. Then AI on top is safe: the model only chooses which metric, which breakdown and which filter; the engine writes the SQL, and Unity Catalog's row and column rules apply to whoever is asking.

  • Genie spaces are curated: a short list of trusted tables or metric views, written instructions, and example questions with their correct answers.
  • Benchmark questions are run before go-live and after every change, so we know the answers are still right.
  • Security flows through: a plant manager in one plant sees only that plant's rows, in a dashboard, in Genie and in the Conversation UI alike.

Questions

Questions leaders ask

Does our data leave our AWS account?

No. Databricks on AWS runs its compute in your account and your VPC, and all tables live in your S3 buckets, encrypted with KMS keys you own. The Databricks control plane manages the workspace; for serverless compute, check the current data-residency terms for your region.

Why Databricks if we already have Glue, EMR and Redshift?

One engine for batch and streaming, one catalog (Unity Catalog) for access, lineage and audit, and AI/BI with Genie on the same governed data. You do not have to switch everything at once: keep what works and put new work on the lakehouse.

What is a metric view?

A definition of a business metric (for example, scrap rate) stored once in Unity Catalog, with its formula, dimensions and filters. Dashboards, Genie and the Conversation UI all use it, so the number is the same everywhere.

What are horizontal assets?

Parts built once and reused by every pipeline and data product: ingestion, metadata, quality, cost, testing and the conversation layer. They are what makes the tenth data product much cheaper than the first.

How are compound recipes and personal data protected?

Recipes and formulation data sit in a restricted catalog with tag-based access; personal data of dealers, fleet drivers and consumers is tagged in silver and masked by Unity Catalog column masks and row filters for every reader, not only in dashboards. Indian data stays in Indian regions and European data in EU regions. Access is audited in system tables and CloudTrail.
