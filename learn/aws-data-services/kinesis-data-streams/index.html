Amazon Kinesis Data Streams

A managed, ordered log of events that many readers can replay, kept from one day up to a year, with no cluster to run.

RoleStreaming and events
StatusGenerally available. Three changes since 2025: records can now be up to 10 MiB; an account-level On-demand Advantage mode (Nov 2025) lowers data prices but bills a floor of 25 MB/s; and streaming tables (Aug 2026) deliver a stream straight into Iceberg tables on S3 Tables, for on-demand streams only.
Verdict on this sitePursue
AzureEvent Hubs

Open sourceApache Kafka (or Redpanda)

Provisioned with 7 days of retention once volume is known; on-demand for the first months.

In plain words

What it is

Amazon Kinesis Data Streams is a managed event log. An event is a small record that says something happened: a curing press reported 182 degrees at 10:04:05, a shopper added a tyre to a basket, a vehicle sensor saw low pressure. Producers write these records into a stream, and the stream keeps them in arrival order for a retention period you choose, from 24 hours up to 365 days. Readers, called consumers, read the records at their own pace. Reading does not delete anything, so two, five or twenty different programs can read the same records, and any of them can go back and read again.

A stream is cut into shards. A shard is a lane of fixed capacity: in provisioned mode each shard accepts up to 1 MB a second or 1,000 records a second of writes, and serves 2 MB a second of reads. Every record carries a partition key, such as a press number, and Kinesis hashes that key to choose the shard. All records with the same key land on the same shard in the same order, which is how you keep each machine's readings in sequence. You either choose the number of shards yourself (provisioned mode) or let AWS scale them for you (on-demand mode).

A concrete example: a tyre plant has about 2,000 presses and mixers, each sending 20 signals every 5 seconds, roughly 8,000 readings a second. A gateway in each plant batches readings and writes them to one Kinesis stream in the Mumbai Region, keyed by press number. A Flink job reads the stream to raise an alert within seconds when a press drifts out of its limits. Separately, a Databricks streaming table reads the very same stream every 15 minutes and appends everything into a bronze Delta table. If the Databricks job breaks for a day, it catches up from its checkpoint.

A conveyor belt with numbered trays that keeps every tray for a week, so any station can walk back along the belt and pick up what it missed.

Big picture

Where it fits

The lit lane is where Kinesis Data Streams works. Boxes on the left feed it; boxes on the right use what it produces.

On the reference platform Kinesis Data Streams is the belt in the move-in lane for anything that arrives as a stream of records rather than as files. Plant gateways running SiteWise Edge on AWS IoT Greengrass write to it directly with an IAM role; IoT Core rules forward connected-tyre messages into it; app backends can write to it through API Gateway or the SDK. It is the split point of the streaming design: the raw event is written once, and every lane reads it from there.

Two lanes read it. The fast lane, Managed Service for Apache Flink, computes windows and limit checks and writes results to DynamoDB, which pushes changes to screens. The platform lane, a Lakeflow streaming table, reads the raw stream on a 15-minute trigger and lands it in bronze with the shard, sequence number and arrival time as envelope columns. Because the platform lane reads the raw stream rather than the fast lane's output, a bug in Flink can never corrupt the number of record.

Kinesis keeps 7 days on this platform. That is enough to rebuild Flink state (the live lane only needs 48 hours) and to survive a long weekend of a broken Databricks job. Bronze keeps everything after that, forever, which is far cheaper than paying the stream to hold years of readings. For sources that only need minutes, the site skips the belt and writes files to S3 through Firehose instead.

How it works

The ideas you need

IdeaWhat it means
ShardA unit of capacity inside a stream: 1 MB a second or 1,000 records a second in, 2 MB a second out, in provisioned mode.
Partition keyA value you attach to each record, such as a press number. Kinesis hashes it to pick the shard, so the same key always keeps its order.
Sequence numberA unique, increasing number Kinesis gives each record within a shard. Readers store it to know where they stopped.
Retention periodHow long records stay readable: 24 hours by default, up to 7 days as extended retention, and up to 365 days as long-term retention.
Shared throughput consumerA reader that polls with GetRecords. All such readers on a shard share its 2 MB a second and five calls a second.
Enhanced fan-outA registered consumer that gets its own 2 MB a second per shard, pushed over HTTP/2 in about 70 milliseconds. Up to 20 per stream, or 50 in On-demand Advantage mode.
Capacity modeProvisioned, where you set the shard count, or on-demand, where AWS scales shards and bills per GB. On-demand has a Standard and an Advantage pricing option.
Iterator ageHow old the last record a reader fetched was when it fetched it. A growing iterator age means the reader is falling behind.
Streaming tablesA 2026 feature that delivers a stream straight into an Apache Iceberg table on S3 Tables, append only, for on-demand streams.

Choosing

When to use it, and when not

Use it when

  • Use it when two or more programs must read the same events, such as a live alert job and a lakehouse loader.
  • Use it when readers must be able to replay the last hours or days after a bug fix or an outage.
  • Use it when order per key matters, for example every reading of one press must be processed in sequence.
  • Use it when the producers are AWS-native, such as IoT Core rules, Greengrass stream manager, DMS or CloudWatch, and you want no cluster to patch.
  • Use it when volume is a few MB a second up to several GB a second and you prefer paying per shard or per GB to sizing brokers.

Avoid it when

  • Avoid it when the source only needs to arrive within minutes and has a single reader; Firehose writing files to S3 is cheaper and simpler.
  • Avoid it when your team and tools already speak Kafka, for example Debezium, Kafka Connect or a partner's Kafka topics; MSK fits better.
  • Avoid it as long-term storage for years of history; keep 7 days in the stream and the rest in bronze tables.
  • Avoid it when you need one consumer to process each message once and then forget it, such as a work queue; that is SQS.

Production grade

What goes wrong in production

ProblemWhat breaksWhat to do
Hot shardOne busy key, such as a single line controller that sends ten times more than others, overloads one shard; writes are throttled and data lags.Pick a partition key with many values (press number, not plant); watch WriteProvisionedThroughputExceeded per shard with enhanced metrics; use on-demand mode while volume is unknown.
Readers fighting over a shardSeveral polling readers share 2 MB a second and five GetRecords calls a second per shard, so adding a third reader slows the first two.Give each important reader enhanced fan-out; keep shared polling for one cheap reader at most.
Reader falls behind past retentionA stalled reader that is more than the retention period behind silently loses records; they expire before it reads them.Alarm on GetRecords.IteratorAgeMilliseconds at a fraction of retention, for example 12 hours on a 7-day stream; keep bronze as the long memory.
Duplicates on retryProducers retry failed PutRecords calls and readers reprocess after a restart, so the same record can arrive twice.Put a unique reading_id in every record and deduplicate on it in bronze or silver; treat delivery as at least once.
Partial batch failuresPutRecords accepts up to 500 records and can fail some of them while the call itself succeeds.Check FailedRecordCount in every response and retry only the failed records with backoff; the Kinesis Producer Library does this for you.
Resharding surprisesSplitting or merging shards closes parent shards and opens children; a home-made reader that ignores the lineage reads out of order or misses data.Use the Kinesis Client Library, Flink's connector or Databricks' source, which follow parent and child shards; avoid writing raw GetRecords loops.
Large recordsRecords can now be up to 10 MiB, but large records eat burst capacity and still count against the 1 MB a second per shard over time.Keep sensor records small and batch them; send big payloads to S3 and put only the object key on the stream.

Alternatives

What to compare it with

AlternativePick the alternative whenPick Kinesis Data Streams when
Amazon MSKYour team already uses Kafka tools such as Debezium, Kafka Connect or ksql clients, or you need topics shared with partners or other clouds.You want no brokers to size, patch or rebalance and your producers are AWS services.
Amazon Data Firehose on its ownThe data only needs to land as files in S3 or a table within minutes and nothing else reads it live.Two readers need the same events, or a reader needs replay and per-key order.
Azure Event HubsThe platform runs on Azure; Event Hubs also speaks the Kafka protocol, so Kafka clients connect without code changes.The platform runs on AWS; cross-cloud traffic adds cost and delay.
Google Pub/SubThe platform runs on Google Cloud and you want a global topic with no partitions to plan.You need strict order per key at high volume without ordering-key limits, on AWS.
Databricks Zerobus IngestA single app stream has exactly one reader, the Delta table, and you want no belt at all.A live lane or a second reader needs the same events, or you need Kinesis-style replay; the site drops it for sensors.
Amazon SQS, SNS or EventBridgeYou need a work queue, fan-out notifications or event routing between services.You need an analytics log: a queue deletes a message once read, so there is no replay, no second reader and no per-key order.

Cost

How you pay

Provisioned mode bills per shard-hour, plus a charge per PUT payload unit (each record rounded up to 25 KB), plus extended retention per shard-hour beyond 24 hours, long-term retention per GB-month beyond 7 days, and enhanced fan-out per consumer-shard-hour plus per GB read. On-demand Standard bills a per-stream hour plus per GB written and read, with no shard planning. On-demand Advantage, an account-wide setting, removes the per-stream charge and lowers per-GB prices, but bills at least 25 MB a second of ingest and of retrieval across all on-demand streams. The site's sizing for the plant stream is about $120 a month provisioned (2 shards, 7 days, 2 dedicated readers) and about $160 on-demand; list price, check your region.

What drives the bill

  • Shard count in provisioned mode, which follows the busier of bytes a second and records a second.
  • Many tiny records: each one is at least one 25 KB PUT payload unit, so batching ten readings into one record cuts the bill.
  • Retention beyond 24 hours, charged per shard-hour up to 7 days and per GB-month after that.
  • Each enhanced fan-out reader, charged per shard-hour and per GB it reads.
  • Delivery to streaming tables or S3, charged per TB delivered.

Cost traps

  • Turning on On-demand Advantage for a small platform: the 25 MB a second floor is far above a plant stream of a few MB a second, and the switch cannot be undone for 24 hours.
  • Leaving on-demand mode on at steady, predictable load; provisioned is usually cheaper once you know the volume.
  • Setting 365-day retention to avoid building bronze; you pay to store years in the stream and still cannot query it with SQL.
  • Forgetting dev and test streams that keep running with shards and retention nobody uses.

Prices change; check the AWS pricing page for your region before deciding.

Security and governance

Keeping it safe

  • Use IAM roles for every producer and reader: the plant gateway gets kinesis:PutRecords on one stream only, Databricks' instance profile or service credential gets read actions only.
  • Turn on server-side encryption with the platform's KMS key; producers and readers then also need kms:GenerateDataKey or kms:Decrypt on that key.
  • Reach Kinesis through an interface VPC endpoint so traffic from Databricks, Flink and Lambda never leaves the private network; add an endpoint policy that names the allowed streams.
  • Use a resource policy on the stream for cross-account readers instead of sharing access keys.
  • CloudTrail records control-plane calls such as CreateStream and UpdateShardCount; data-plane calls are not logged by default.
  • If records carry personal data, such as a driver ID from a vehicle, keep retention short and mask the field in silver.

FAQ

Questions people ask

What is the difference between Kinesis Data Streams and Firehose?

Data Streams is a log that keeps records so many readers can process them, in order, and replay them. Firehose is a delivery service that buffers records and writes them as files or table rows into S3, Redshift or other destinations. They are often used together: Firehose can read from a Kinesis stream.

Can Databricks read Kinesis directly?

Yes. Structured Streaming and Lakeflow streaming tables have a Kinesis source, and enhanced fan-out is recommended. On the site's platform a streaming table reads the stream every 15 minutes and stops, so nothing runs continuously. Check that the trigger you want works with the Kinesis source on your runtime.

Should I use provisioned or on-demand mode?

Start on-demand while you do not know the volume, then switch to provisioned once traffic is steady, because provisioned is usually cheaper at predictable load. You can switch a stream between modes twice in 24 hours. Streaming tables delivery needs on-demand mode.

How long should a Kinesis stream keep data?

Long enough to rebuild your stream processors and to survive a reader being broken over a weekend, typically 7 days. Keep longer history in bronze tables, which are far cheaper and can be queried with SQL.

Kinesis or MSK?

Kinesis when you want a managed stream with no cluster and AWS-native producers; MSK when you already speak Kafka, need Kafka Connect or Debezium, or want the same tool on every cloud.
