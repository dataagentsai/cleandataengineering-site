3b · The 10-minute picture, part 1

Only three kinds of AI application

The kind depends on one thing: who decides the next step? Nobody, your code, or the model. Each kind needs more of the harness than the one before, so choose the simplest kind that does the job.

Legend: green circle = a model call · blue box = your code or a tool.

How the ten shapes fit the three kinds

The assurance and harness catalogs use ten shapes. Each one is a kind plus a modifier, so learners only need three kinds first and meet the shapes when they zoom in.

3c · The 10-minute picture, part 2

What are autonomous agents?

5-second answer: an autonomous agent is an agent nobody is watching. It starts on an event or a schedule, runs for hours or days, and acts on its own within limits someone set in advance.

Autonomy isn't a fourth kind. It's a second dial. The kind says who decides the next step; autonomy says how much a person checks. A scheduled workflow can be autonomous; a chat agent that asks before every action isn't.

Five levels of autonomy

LevelWhat the AI doesWhat the person doesExample
0 · AnswerAnswers one questionReads it and decidesSummarise this email
1 · SuggestProposes an actionTakes the actionA copilot drafts a reply
2 · Act with approvalActs, but asks before anything importantApproves or rejectsSupport agent asks before a refund
3 · Act within limitsActs on its own inside a budget and rulesReviews afterwardsAgent resolves small claims, logs every step
4 · Run unattendedStarts itself, runs for hours or days, repairs itselfSets goals and limits, reads the reportOps agent handling incidents overnight

The same five levels describe the Autopilot idea on the Data Engineering path, so the two paths share one vocabulary.

What autonomous agents bring a business

Each benefit comes with the number you'd measure to prove it. A benefit you can't measure is a claim, not a benefit.

BenefitIn the claims exampleHow you'd prove it
Work doesn't wait for office hoursA claim filed at 11pm is checked and acknowledged at 11pm, not the next morningTime to first response
Faster from start to finishSmall, simple claims settled in minutes instead of daysCycle time per claim
Volume spikes without hiringA storm triples claims for a week; the queue doesn'tBacklog size, cases per person
People do the judgement workAdjusters handle disputed and large claims; the agent does the routine onesShare of staff time spent on exceptions
The same rules every timeEvery claim is checked against the same policy, and every step is loggedRule-violation rate, audit findings
Lower cost per unit of workCost per resolved claim drops, especially when rules handle what they can before the model is calledCost per resolved claim
Problems caught at 3amAn ops agent spots a failing payment run overnight and fixes or escalates itTime to detect, time to resolve

Worth it when the work is…

  • high volume and repetitive
  • governed by clear rules
  • reversible, or cheap to get wrong
  • measurable: you can tell good from bad

Not worth it (yet) when the work is…

  • rare, or different every time
  • irreversible and high-stakes
  • impossible to judge: no way to tell if it went well
  • cheaper with a simple rule or a workflow

Ties to the X/Y/Z idea from the funding work: autonomous agents take over the repeated work, so people spend their time on the part only they can do.

4 · The 10-minute picture, part 3

The harness answers seven questions

Three jobs, seven questions. Every part of the harness answers exactly one of them. The first job matches what every vendor guide calls an agent (model, instructions, tools, loop); the other two are what production adds.

Do the work what the guides call "the agent"

1 · How does it decide?

  • Routing: does this even need the model?
  • Model calls: which model, through which door
  • Steps: the fixed path of a workflow
  • The loop: plan, act, check, stop
  • Sub-agents and handoffs: when one agent gives work to another

2 · What does it know?

  • Instructions (prompts, versioned)
  • Context: what goes into the window
  • Knowledge: finding the right documents
  • Memory: what it remembers next time

3 · What can it do?

  • Tools: actions, MCP, safe writes, sandboxed code
Earn trust what production adds, part 1

4 · Is it safe?

  • Guardrails, including prompt injection
  • Identity and permissions
  • Privacy and personal data
  • Human approval and handoff
  • Audit trail

5 · Does it keep working?

  • Failure handling: retries, fallbacks, no double actions
  • Durable state: it crashed at step 7, now what?
  • Speed and scale: latency, load, limits, caching
  • Availability and recovery
Get better what production adds, part 2

6 · Can I see what it did, and what it cost?

  • Tracing and replay
  • Cost

7 · Is it good, and can I change it safely?

  • Evaluation: before release, in production, from feedback
  • Release and versioning: prompt, model and config together

Around the harness, drawn outside the box: the front door (channels), your business systems and data, ordinary software engineering, and the LLM Platform.

4b · The 50-minute view

Four ways to slice the same list

People draw agent architecture in different ways: core vs cross-cutting, functional vs non-functional, client vs server vs systems. They aren't competing models. They're columns on one list, and three things fall out of putting them side by side.

1 · The splits mostly agree

Core = functional = Do the work. Cross-cutting = non-functional = Earn trust and Get better. One split teaches both. Two honest exceptions: human approval is a business rule (functional) that works as a safety gate, and guardrails are non-functional but sit at fixed points in the path.

2 · The harness grows with the kind

A single call needs 13 parts (plus 4 sometimes). A workflow needs 18. An agent needs 20, and sub-agents when there are many. That's the 50-minute lesson: start with one call, add parts as you move up a kind.

3 · Where each part lives

Server holds almost everything, because it is the harness. Client UI shows up at four moments: signing in, watching the answer stream, approving an action, and giving feedback (plus honest error messages and consent). Systems are what the harness leans on: the LLM Platform, business systems, data stores, identity provider, observability backend, CI/CD.

6 · Around the harness

What sits outside the box

Your system

  • Front door: chat, email, phone, apps, streaming the answer
  • Business systems and data: what the tools reach
  • It's still software: APIs and typed contracts, config and secrets, CI/CD, infrastructure as code, the test pyramid

LLM Platform

  • Models: which brain
  • Providers: who trains and serves it
  • Access and gateway: one door, routing, caching, quotas
  • Compute: where it physically runs
  • The AI Universe page explains the layers in detail

Non-functional requirements and cross-cutting concerns aren't a fourth box. They're the "Earn trust" and "Get better" jobs. Architects who want the formal names (ISO 25010: security, reliability, performance, and so on) get a mapping table at the 5-hour level. Beginners never need it.

8 · Decide together

Open questions

  1. Metaphor: the car, the body, or none?The car matches your AI Universe line ("the engine, not the car"). Google uses a body (brain, hands, nervous system). A metaphor makes doodles easy but can be stretched too far.
  2. Seven questions: right number?Could go to six by merging 6 and 7 ("Can I see it and improve it?").
  3. Do the work / Earn trust / Get better: are those the right three job names?Alternatives: Build / Protect / Improve, or Think & act / Stay safe & steady / Learn.
  4. Model calls: inside the harness, or drawn as the bridge between the boxes?Inside keeps the harness complete; a bridge makes the two-box picture clearer.
  5. Sub-agents: their own part (as drafted), or a variant of the loop?Their own part matches what learners search for; a variant keeps question 1 shorter.
  6. Are the formal NFR names shown to learners at all?Proposed: only at the 5-hour level, as a mapping table for architects.
