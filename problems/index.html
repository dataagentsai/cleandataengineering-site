The hardest unsolved problems, and which ones one person can test

79 problems nobody has solved yet, from the core of AI to India's own. Each one has its own page with a picture of the problem, why it is still open, an experiment one person could run in about 8 weeks, and a verdict.

How the verdict is decided. Every problem was put through the same four questions:

  1. Can it be measured? There is a number that shows you did better, such as an error rate, a catch rate or a cost per answer.
  2. Is it cheap to try? It runs on a laptop, free open models and open data, or under about ₹50,000 of rented GPU time.
  3. Is there a public yardstick? A benchmark or dataset others use, so your result can be compared and believed.
  4. Would a small win get noticed? A lab, a funder or a customer would care about a 10% improvement, not only a full solution.

Four yeses means Solo: strong. Two or three means Solo: possible. Fewer means Follow, don't build.

The pattern across all 79. The problems one person can really test are mostly about measurement: building the test, the dataset or the honest comparison a field is missing. Labs have compute; what they lack is careful outside measurement, and India's problems get the least of it.
