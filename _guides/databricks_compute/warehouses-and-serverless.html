Warehouses and serverless

SQL warehouses in detail

A SQL warehouse is compute built only for SQL: BI dashboards, SQL editor, ad hoc queries, many users at once. It runs Spark underneath but hides it. You cannot run Python on it and you cannot tune most Spark settings. In return you get Photon on by default, a query queue, and concurrency scaling.

Predictive IO in plain words. Instead of reading a whole file and then filtering, the engine learns which parts of files a query will actually need and reads only those. It acts like an automatic index. It speeds up selective queries and MERGE/UPDATE/DELETE on Delta.

Intelligent workload management in plain words. Before a query runs, it is scored as cheap or heavy. Cheap queries slip through, heavy ones get capacity added early. This stops one huge query from blocking a dashboard.

Warehouses and serverless

Serverless that is not a SQL warehouse

Serverless notebooks, serverless jobs and serverless pipelines give you Spark without a cluster. You never pick machines, runtime or autoscaling. You write code, and Databricks supplies compute from a warm pool.

Performance optimized vs standard mode

Serverless jobs and pipelines offer two modes. It is the same engine with a different promise about speed versus price.

Performance optimizedStandard
Start-upSeconds, VMs held readyMinutes, VMs allocated on demand
ScalingAggressive, scales fastMore conservative
Cost per hourHigherLower
Good forInteractive work, jobs with tight deadlines, short jobs where start-up dominatesNightly batch where nobody is waiting
VerdictPursue for notebooks and urgent jobsPursue for relaxed overnight jobs to save money

Serverless notebooks always behave as performance optimized because a person is waiting.

What you give up on serverless

  • No choice of machine type, so no storage-optimized or memory-optimized picks.
  • No init scripts or custom JARs on the machines. Libraries come through the environment settings.
  • Most Spark settings are locked. Spark caching calls such as cache() and persist() are limited.
  • Unity Catalog is required, so legacy Hive metastore and DBFS mounts do not work.
  • You cannot SSH or inspect a worker.

What you gain. No capacity planning, instant start, nothing idle to forget, automatic upgrades, and the usual outcome that total cost goes down for bursty or short work.

Warehouses and serverless

Who owns serverless: accounts, workspaces, prod and dev, BU1 and BU2

There is no single "serverless switch" that creates one serverless thing. Serverless is a capability that an account turns on, and the compute itself is created per workspace, on demand, and isolated per customer. These are the layers, from the top.

Your questionAnswer
At what level does the client turn serverless on?Two levels. The account admin accepts the serverless terms, creates network connectivity configs (NCC) and budget policies. Then each workspace admin allows serverless SQL warehouses, notebooks and jobs for that workspace. Serverless SQL warehouses are individual objects you create and size inside a workspace. Serverless notebooks and jobs have no object to create; compute appears when you run.
Will client A and client B get different serverless?Yes, always. They are different accounts, and Databricks isolates each customer's workloads from every other customer's. Nothing is shared at the VM level.
Can one client have two serverless, prod and dev?Yes. Use separate workspaces for prod and dev (strong boundary: access, networking, audit, catalog binding), and separate warehouses inside each. Each workspace's serverless is independent and has its own usage trail.
One for BU1 and one for BU2?Yes, in two ways. Hard split: a workspace per BU (or per BU and environment). Soft split: one workspace, one warehouse per BU, plus a serverless budget policy per BU that tags all of their serverless usage. Hard split gives clean isolation and simple bill reading. Soft split gives less sprawl but needs tag discipline.
